\begin{abstract}
Multi-access edge computing (MEC) micro-datacenters place a handful of
virtualized servers at the network edge, where they are exposed to
crypto-ransomware campaigns that move laterally, steal data, and then encrypt it
to deny service. Unlike a classical breach, a ransomware incident is
survivable: locked servers can be restored from immutable backups, so its true
cost is the transient loss of confidentiality, service, and data recency accrued
during the race between the attacker's kill-chain and the defender's
detect-and-restore response. We develop CRADLE, a continuous-time Markov reward
model, drawn as a stochastic reward net, of an ordered
compromise--exfiltrate--encrypt--lock kill-chain on a MEC micro-datacenter,
defended by an endpoint monitor whose detection hazard grows with the
encryption and exfiltration footprint and by an isolate-and-restore response
with explicit recovery-point and recovery-time semantics. We derive transient
state probabilities, accumulated-reward security losses, first-passage
detection and resolution distributions, and closed per-incident expectations
via the fundamental matrix, and we prove the footprint-aware detection breaks
the memoryless recovery law of prior uniform-recovery models. A defender
co-design procedure jointly tunes detection sensitivity and backup cadence to
minimize expected total cost, tracing a security-versus-spend Pareto frontier.
An independent Gillespie simulation validates every analytic result. The study
yields actionable, and at times counterintuitive, guidance for edge ransomware
resilience.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{M}{ulti-access} edge computing (MEC) pushes computation to
micro-datacenters (MEDCs) of a few virtualized servers placed beside base
stations, delivering the low latency that immersive, industrial and vehicular
applications demand~\cite{mao2017survey,etsi_mec}. That proximity is also an
attack surface: an MEDC sits in a physically and administratively exposed
location, hosts multi-tenant virtual machines and containers, and is a natural
staging point for adversaries seeking the data and control flows that traverse
the edge~\cite{roman2018survey}. The dominant monetized threat against such
fleets today is \emph{crypto-ransomware}: an adversary gains an initial
foothold, moves laterally between servers, exfiltrates data for double-extortion
leverage, and then encrypts data at rest to deny service until a ransom is
paid~\cite{kharraz2015gordian,alrimy2018ransomware}.

Quantitative stochastic models have proven invaluable for reasoning about the
security and dependability of such systems before deployment. The MEDC-under-
attack model of Liu \emph{et al.}~\cite{liu2021transient} is the closest
antecedent: it analyses a lateral-movement campaign on an MEDC as an absorbing
continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) and computes transient infection,
exfiltration and loss metrics. That model, however, makes two simplifications
that matter for ransomware. First, it treats \emph{recovery} as a single uniform
rate to an absorbing ``defense-deployed'' state, independent of how far the
attack has progressed---so the recovery probability is exactly memoryless. In
reality, detection is driven by the attacker's own noise: encryption produces a
high-entropy I/O storm and mass data egress that an endpoint monitor sees
precisely \emph{because} the campaign is advancing. Second, it has no notion of
\emph{data-at-rest denial and its reversal}: encryption is not modelled as a
recoverable service-denial state, and there is no backup-and-restore layer with
recovery-point (RPO, how much data is lost) or recovery-time (RTO, how long
restoration takes) semantics---exactly the levers a defender actually controls.

This paper closes that gap. We propose \textbf{CRADLE} (\emph{Crypto-Ransomware
Attack: Detection-race, Loss and restore-Economics}), a transient Markov reward
model, expressed as a stochastic reward net (SRN), of a crypto-ransomware
double-extortion kill-chain on an MEDC, defended by a footprint-aware endpoint
detection-and-response (EDR) monitor and an immutable-backup isolate-and-restore
response. A ransomware incident in CRADLE is \emph{survivable}: every trajectory
is eventually restored, and the object of study is the transient security and
dependability loss---confidentiality, service availability, data recency, and
downtime---accumulated while the kill-chain races the response.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An ordered double-extortion ransomware SRN} (Section~\ref{sec:model})
whose per-server kill-chain is \emph{Vulnerable$\to$Compromised$\to$Exfiltrating
$\to$Encrypting$\to$Locked}, with lateral movement scaling in the active
footprint, and in which encryption is a \emph{recoverable} service-denial state
rather than an absorbing breach.
\item \textbf{A footprint-aware detection hazard} keyed on the
encryption/exfiltration I/O footprint, coupled to an \textbf{isolate-and-restore
response with explicit RPO/RTO} and imperfect restore coverage
(Section~\ref{sec:arch}--\ref{sec:model}). We prove that this coupling breaks the
memoryless recovery law of uniform-recovery models: the resolution curve is
S-shaped, not exponential.
\item \textbf{Exact transient and per-incident measures}
(Section~\ref{sec:measures}): transient probabilities and accumulated-reward
losses via matrix exponentials, first-passage detection and resolution
distributions, and closed per-incident expectations (mean time-to-detection,
mean time-to-resolution, expected datasets exfiltrated, denial server-days, and
data loss at restore) via the fundamental matrix.
\item \textbf{A defender co-design algorithm} (Section~\ref{sec:algo}) that
jointly optimizes detection sensitivity and backup cadence to minimize expected
total cost, tracing the security-versus-operational-spend Pareto frontier and
quantifying the penalty of a detection-blind backup schedule.
\item \textbf{A dual analytic/simulation validation}
(Section~\ref{sec:valid}) and a numerical study (Section~\ref{sec:results})
that surface actionable design guidance, including the counterintuitive result
that sharper detection lets a defender back up \emph{less} often.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Stochastic dependability and security modelling.} Markov reward models,
stochastic Petri/reward nets and semi-Markov processes are the standard vehicles
for quantifying availability, reliability and security of computing
systems~\cite{trivedi2016probability,trivedi2017reliability,nicol2004modelbased,
muppala1994srn}. Trivedi and co-workers pioneered the unified treatment of
performance, dependability and security (``performability'' and quantitative
security) that this paper follows. The dependability/security taxonomy of
Avi\v{z}ienis \emph{et al.}~\cite{avizienis2004basic} frames encryption-based
denial as a combined integrity-and-availability fault whose handling
(detection, isolation, recovery) is precisely what CRADLE quantifies.
Intrusion-tolerance models in this lineage---for instance the state-transition
security model of Madan \emph{et al.}~\cite{madan2004method}, which derives mean
time to security failure from an absorbing CTMC---established the reward- and
first-passage-based measures that we lift here from a single host to a
footprint-coupled MEDC fleet with data-centric restore.

\textbf{MEDC and edge under attack.} Liu \emph{et al.}~\cite{liu2021transient}
model a lateral-movement attack on an MEDC as an absorbing CTMC with a uniform
recovery to a ``good'' state; it is our direct antecedent and baseline. A
subsequent line models \emph{self-propagating} attacks on micro-segmented edge
fabrics with two competing absorbing outcomes (contained vs.\ irreversibly
breached) and detection hazards that grow with the compromised-node
count~\cite{roman2018survey}. CRADLE differs in threat model and objective: our
detection hazard is keyed on the \emph{encryption/exfiltration} footprint rather
than compromised-node count; encryption is a \emph{recoverable} denial state,
not a terminal breach; and our response is a backup \emph{restore} with RPO/RTO
data-loss economics rather than a re-image.

\textbf{Ransomware analysis.} Empirical and systems work dissects ransomware
behaviour and defenses---file-system self-healing, decoy monitoring and
behavioural detection~\cite{kharraz2015gordian,alrimy2018ransomware,
continella2016shieldfs}. These are measurement/mechanism studies; to our
knowledge no prior transient Markov reward model captures the
exfiltrate-then-encrypt kill-chain together with detection and backup-restore
economics.

\textbf{Moving-target defense and proactive recovery.} MTD (IP shuffling, VM
migration, re-imaging) and proactive/rejuvenation-based recovery reset attacker
progress on a timer or a trigger~\cite{jajodia2011mtd,huang1995rejuvenation}. Our
restore is conceptually a triggered reset, but it is \emph{data-centric}: what is
restored is encrypted data from an immutable backup, and the residual cost is the
recovery point, an aspect these works do not model.

\textbf{Numerical methods.} We use the matrix exponential for transient
solutions, Jensen's uniformization as an independent
cross-check~\cite{reibman1988transient}, a Van~Loan-style augmented exponential
for accumulated rewards~\cite{vanloan1978,moler2003nineteen}, the fundamental
matrix for per-incident expectations, and Gillespie's exact stochastic
simulation for validation~\cite{gillespie1977exact}.

\section{System Architecture and Threat Model}\label{sec:arch}

\subsection{Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} depicts the system. An MEDC hosts $m$ MEC servers behind one
or more base stations, each running a virtualization stack (hypervisor or
container engine) that instantiates tenant services close to endpoint and
IoT/CPS devices. Two defensive planes overlay the compute fabric. An
\emph{EDR monitor} continuously observes host and network telemetry; its alert
hazard rises with the volume of ransomware-indicative activity---high-entropy
write bursts from encryption and anomalous data egress from exfiltration. An
\emph{immutable-backup} subsystem periodically snapshots server data to
write-once storage; upon an incident it drives an \emph{isolate-and-restore}
response that network-isolates the MEDC (halting lateral movement and
exfiltration) and restores servers from the last good snapshot.

\subsection{Threat model}
The adversary follows a ransomware double-extortion kill-chain. It obtains
initial access to one server, then propagates \emph{laterally} (the more servers
it controls, the faster it spreads). On a controlled server it \emph{exfiltrates}
data (confidentiality loss and extortion leverage) and only \emph{then}
\emph{encrypts} it, completing to a \emph{locked} state that denies service. The
defender does not know it is under attack until the EDR fires; detection is
imperfect and its timeliness depends on the attacker's footprint. We assume
network isolation is effective once triggered (halting further spread and
egress), that in-flight host-local encryption may still complete before restore,
and that immutable backups are not themselves corruptible (a design goal of
modern write-once backup). We do not model the ransom-payment decision: CRADLE is
a defender-side resilience model whose premise is restore-not-pay.

\section{The CRADLE Stochastic Reward Net}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{State space}
The MEDC has $m$ servers, each in exactly one kill-chain stage. A transient
(operational) state is the tuple
\begin{equation}
s=(a,x,b,c,d),
\end{equation}
where $a,x,b,c$ count servers that are \emph{Compromised}, \emph{Exfiltrating},
\emph{Encrypting} and \emph{Locked}, and $d\in\{0,1\}$ is the campaign phase:
$\stealth$ (stealth/undetected) or $\response$ (response/contained). The number
of clean \emph{Vulnerable} servers is $V=m-p$ with footprint $p=a+x+b+c$, and the
\emph{active} (non-locked) foothold is $h=a+x+b$. Validity requires
$a,x,b,c\ge0$ and $p\le m$; the stealth phase exists for every lattice point,
the response phase only for $p\ge1$ (a campaign must exist to be responded to).
A single absorbing state $\textsc{Good}$ represents the fully restored,
incident-resolved MEDC. Table~\ref{tab:states} summarizes the encoding. The
lattice has $\binom{m+4}{4}$ points per phase, giving
$\Cnt$ transient states $+1$ for the base case $m=\Cm$.

\subsection{Transition rules}
Fig.~\ref{fig:srn} shows the kill-chain and phase automaton; the aggregate rate
laws (verified arc-by-arc against the reachability graph) are in
Table~\ref{tab:trans}. In the stealth phase, a clean server is first compromised
by lateral movement at a rate proportional to the active foothold $h$ (epidemic
spread), inheriting the footprint-scaling of~\cite{liu2021transient}; a
compromised server begins exfiltration at rate $\epsilon$ each; an exfiltrating
server completes (one dataset stolen) and \emph{then} begins encryption at rate
$\phi$; an encrypting server completes to Locked at rate $\rho$. The campaign is
detected---moving to the response phase---at the footprint-aware hazard
\begin{equation}
\delta(s)=\delta_0+\kappa_a a+\kappa_x x+\kappa_b b+\kappa_c c,\qquad
\delta(s)\!=\!0 \text{ if } p{=}0,
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
with $\kappa_c>\kappa_b>\kappa_x\ge\kappa_a$: locked servers raise user-visible
alarms, encryption is a noisy I/O storm, exfiltration leaves an egress
signature, and a bare foothold is nearly silent. In the response phase, network
isolation halts lateral movement, exfiltration and new encryption onsets;
host-local encryption already running still completes ($b\to c$ at $\rho$), and
the restore fires to $\textsc{Good}$ at the state-dependent rate
\begin{equation}
\eta(c)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{RTO}_0+\mathrm{RTO}_1\,c},
\label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the recovery time grows with the number of locked servers to be restored.
With restore coverage $\mathrm{cov}_r\in(0,1]$, a fraction $1-\mathrm{cov}_r$ of
restores are incomplete and leave a residual foothold, returning the campaign to
$(1,0,0,0,0)$.

\section{Security--Dependability Measures}\label{sec:measures}
Let $\pi_0$ be the initial distribution (all mass on the clean state
$(0,0,0,0,0)$) and $\pi(t)=\pi_0 e^{Qt}$ the transient distribution.

\textbf{Transient marginals.} The expected number of servers in a stage is a
reward-weighted marginal, e.g.\ $\E[c(t)]=\sum_{s}c_s\,\pi_s(t)$; the expected
available service fraction is $A(t)=1-\E[c(t)]/m$, and the probability of service
denial is $\Prob(c(t)\ge1)$.

\textbf{Accumulated-reward losses.} For a reward-rate vector $r$, the expected
accumulated reward to time $t$ is $L_r(t)=\int_0^t r^\top\pi(u)\,du$, obtained
without quadrature from the augmented exponential
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}\pi(t)\\ \mathbf{A}(t)\end{bmatrix}
=\exp\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}Q^\top & 0\\ I & 0\end{bmatrix}t\right)
\begin{bmatrix}\pi_0\\ 0\end{bmatrix},\quad
L_r(t)=r^\top\mathbf{A}(t).
\label{eq:accum}
\end{equation}
We instantiate $r$ as the locked count $c$ (service-denial server-days), the
exfiltration-completion rate $\phi x$ restricted to stealth (datasets stolen),
and the restore-flux data loss $\eta(c)w_L c$ restricted to response (data lost
at restore, an RPO quantity).

\textbf{First-passage distributions.} Because a trajectory is stealthy exactly
until detection, the detection CDF is
$\Prob(\text{detected}\le t)=1-\sum_{s:d_s=0}\pi_s(t)$, and the resolution
(restore) CDF is $\Prob(\text{resolved}\le t)=\pi_{\textsc{Good}}(t)$.

\textbf{Per-incident expectations (fundamental matrix).} Let $U$ be $Q$
restricted to $\mathcal{S}$. The expected sojourn vector before absorption is
\begin{equation}
\tau^\top=\pi_{0,\mathcal{S}}^\top(-U)^{-1},
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
from which every per-incident total is the linear functional $\tau^\top r$:
mean time-to-detection $\mathrm{MTTD}=\sum_{d_s=0}\tau_s$, mean response duration
$\sum_{d_s=1}\tau_s$, mean time-to-resolution
$\mathrm{MTTR}=\sum_s\tau_s$, expected datasets exfiltrated
$\phi\sum_{d_s=0}x_s\tau_s$, expected denial server-days $\sum_s c_s\tau_s$, and
expected data loss $w_L\sum_{d_s=1}\eta(c_s)c_s\tau_s$. The identity
$\mathrm{MTTR}=\mathrm{MTTD}+\mathbb{E}[\text{response}]$ holds exactly.

\textbf{Structural result (non-memoryless recovery).} In a uniform-recovery
model where every transient state exits to the absorbing state at a single rate
$\gamma$, the resolution CDF is exactly $1-e^{-\gamma t}$. CRADLE is
qualitatively different, as the following makes precise.

\begin{lemma}[Non-memoryless resolution]\label{lem:nonexp}
For any parameters with $\delta_0\!\ge\!0$ and $\eta(\cdot)\!<\!\infty$, the
time-to-resolution $T_R$ (first passage from the clean state to $\textsc{Good}$)
is a phase-type random variable with density $f_{T_R}(0)=0$. In particular
$T_R$ is not exponentially distributed, and the resolution CDF is not of the
form $1-e^{-\gamma t}$ for any $\gamma>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Absorption into $\textsc{Good}$ occurs only from response states ($d{=}1$), which
are reachable only through the detection event ($d\!:\!0\!\to\!1$); no arc leads
from a stealth state directly to $\textsc{Good}$. Hence every path to absorption
visits at least two distinct transient states, so $T_R\ge T_1+T_2$ with $T_1$
the (positive) time to detection and $T_2$ the (positive) residual to restore.
As a first passage in a finite CTMC starting from a single transient state,
$T_R$ is phase-type; because at least two phases are traversed with probability
one, its density at the origin vanishes, $f_{T_R}(0^+)=0$. The exponential
density is $\gamma e^{-\gamma t}$ with $f(0^+)=\gamma>0$, so $T_R$ cannot be
exponential.
\end{proof}

The stealth window thus imprints an initial ``dead time'' on the resolution
curve, making it S-shaped; the coupling of detection to attack progress is
exactly what a uniform-recovery model~\cite{liu2021transient} cannot represent.
Section~\ref{sec:results} quantifies the departure.

\textbf{Security--dependability cost.} We weight the incident harms into a
single security-dependability loss and add the defender's operational spend:
\begin{align}
S &= c_{\mathrm{br}}\E[\text{exfil}]+c_{\mathrm{dn}}\E[\text{denial}]
   +c_{\mathrm{dl}}\E[\text{loss}]+c_{\mathrm{rt}}\E[\text{response}],\\
J &= S + \underbrace{C_{\mathrm{mon}}(\sigma_D)+C_{\mathrm{bkp}}(\tau_b)}_{O},
\label{eq:cost}
\end{align}
where $\sigma_D$ and $\tau_b$ are the defender levers introduced next.

\section{Solution Algorithms and Defender Co-Design}\label{sec:algo}

\subsection{Generator synthesis and exact solution}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:gen} builds $Q$; the transient distribution and accumulated
rewards follow from \eqref{eq:accum} by forming a single base-step propagator
$e^{Q\Delta t}$ and composing it along a uniform grid (exact, no step error). The
per-incident totals follow from the one linear solve~\eqref{eq:tau}. We
cross-check the matrix exponential against Jensen's uniformization.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{CRADLE generator synthesis}
\label{alg:gen}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE parameters $\theta$, size $m$
\STATE enumerate $\mathcal{S}=\{(a,x,b,c,d): a{+}x{+}b{+}c\le m,\ d\in\{0,1\},\
(d{=}1\Rightarrow p\ge1)\}$
\STATE $Q\gets 0$
\FORALL{$s=(a,x,b,c,d)\in\mathcal{S}$}
  \FORALL{$(s',\lambda)$ in \textsc{Transitions}$(s;\theta)$ (Table~\ref{tab:trans})}
    \STATE $Q_{s,s'}\gets Q_{s,s'}+\lambda$;\quad $Q_{s,s}\gets Q_{s,s}-\lambda$
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{assert} $\max_s|\sum_{s'}Q_{s,s'}|<\varepsilon$
\RETURN $Q$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Defender co-design}
The defender holds two orthogonal levers. \emph{Detection sensitivity}
$\sigma_D\ge1$ scales the footprint coefficients
$(\kappa_x,\kappa_b,\kappa_c)\mapsto\sigma_D(\kappa_x,\kappa_b,\kappa_c)$---a
sharper EDR shortens the stealth window but costs more to run,
$C_{\mathrm{mon}}(\sigma_D)=c_{\mathrm{mon}}\sigma_D$. \emph{Backup cadence}
$\tau_b$ (hours) sets the recovery point: the data loss per locked server scales
with $\tau_b$ (i.e.\ $w_L\propto\tau_b$), while the backup/storage overhead scales
with the frequency, $C_{\mathrm{bkp}}(\tau_b)=c_{\mathrm{bkp}}\,\tau_{b0}/\tau_b$.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:codesign} minimizes $J(\sigma_D,\tau_b)$ of \eqref{eq:cost}:
it maps the surface on a grid, then polishes with alternating one-dimensional
golden-section searches (each objective evaluation is one
solve~\eqref{eq:tau}). Sweeping the levers and retaining non-dominated
$(O,S)$ pairs yields the Pareto frontier.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Detection/backup co-design}
\label{alg:codesign}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE base parameters $\theta$, cost config, lever boxes
$[\sigma_{lo},\sigma_{hi}],[\tau_{lo},\tau_{hi}]$
\STATE evaluate $J$ on a $\sigma_D\times\tau_b$ grid via
Alg.~\ref{alg:gen}+\eqref{eq:tau}; set $(\sigma_D,\tau_b)\gets$ grid minimizer
\REPEAT
  \STATE $\tau_b\gets$ \textsc{GoldenMin}$_{\tau}\ J(\sigma_D,\cdot)$
  \STATE $\sigma_D\gets$ \textsc{GoldenMin}$_{\sigma}\ J(\cdot,\tau_b)$
\UNTIL{converged}
\STATE \textbf{return} $(\sigma_D^\star,\tau_b^\star)$, $J^\star$, and the
non-dominated frontier $\{(O,S)\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Independent simulation}
Algorithm-agnostic validation is provided by an exact Gillespie
simulation~\cite{gillespie1977exact} that consumes only the per-state transition
list, evolves each trajectory clean-to-restored, and rebuilds the transient
probabilities, CDFs and per-incident totals with confidence intervals.

The counterintuitive finding is in the levers themselves
(Figs.~\ref{fig:levsig}--\ref{fig:levtau}). A detection-blind operator, unable to
reduce the number of servers that get locked, over-invests in \emph{frequent}
backups to fight data loss and still pays a penalty of $\Cblindpen\%$ relative to
the co-design. The co-designer instead sharpens detection so that far fewer
servers ever reach the locked state---cutting expected denial by $\Cdenialdroppct\%$---and
can therefore \emph{relax} the backup interval, saving storage overhead.
\emph{Better detection buys looser backups}: the two levers are economic
substitutes, and treating them independently is strictly sub-optimal.

\subsection{Robustness to imperfect restore}
Real restores sometimes fail to fully evict the adversary---a re-imaged or
restored server can be re-infected from a missed persistence foothold, and
double-extortion crews often re-encrypt precisely to punish incomplete recovery.
Fig.~\ref{fig:cov} sweeps the restore coverage $\mathrm{cov}_r$ from perfect down
to $0.6$. As coverage degrades, a fraction of incidents relapse into a fresh
(smaller) campaign before finally resolving, and both the mean
time-to-resolution and the expected data loss inflate super-linearly---by
$\CcovMttrPct\%$ and $\CcovLossPct\%$ respectively at $\mathrm{cov}_r=0.7$. The
practical corollary is that restore \emph{quality} (verified, immutable, fully
evicting) is not a second-order concern: a cheap-but-leaky restore can erase the
benefit of an otherwise well-tuned detection/backup co-design.

\subsection{Discussion and design guidance}
Three guidelines follow. (1) \emph{Instrument for the crypto footprint.}
Because detection hazard rises with encryption I/O, tuning the EDR toward
high-entropy write bursts yields the steepest security return.
(2) \emph{Co-design detection and backups.} They are substitutes; sharper
detection prevents locking and lets backups be relaxed, so budgeting them
separately over-spends. (3) \emph{Size is not the risk.} Per-incident harm
saturates with fleet size, so consolidation for efficiency does not materially
raise ransomware exposure once detection and restore are in place.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
CRADLE assumes exponential stage sojourns; heavy-tailed encryption or restore
times would be better captured by a semi-Markov or phase-type refinement, which
the accumulated-reward and fundamental-matrix machinery accommodates directly.
We assume immutable backups are incorruptible and isolation is effective;
relaxing these adds arcs but not new formalism. The cost weights are
illustrative---the framework consumes any weights, and the qualitative
guidance (substitutability of detection and backups, saturation with size) is
weight-robust across the sweeps we ran. Finally, we exclude the ransom-payment
game by design; CRADLE models the resilience of a restore-not-pay defender.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
We presented CRADLE, a transient stochastic-reward-net model of a
crypto-ransomware double-extortion kill-chain on a MEC micro-datacenter,
defended by a footprint-aware endpoint monitor and an immutable-backup
isolate-and-restore response. Unlike prior edge-under-attack models, CRADLE
treats encryption as a recoverable service-denial state rather than an absorbing
breach, orders exfiltration before encryption to capture double extortion, ties
the detection hazard to the encryption and exfiltration footprint, and endows the
response with explicit recovery-point and recovery-time economics. From one
generator we obtained transient stage and service-denial probabilities,
accumulated-reward losses in confidentiality, availability and data recency,
first-passage detection and resolution distributions, and closed per-incident
expectations via the fundamental matrix. We proved and demonstrated that
coupling detection to attack progress makes the resolution law non-memoryless, a
qualitative departure from uniform-recovery models. A co-design procedure
jointly tuned detection sensitivity and backup cadence to minimize expected total
cost, revealing a Pareto frontier and the substitutability of the two levers---so
that sharper detection lets a defender back up less often---while showing that
per-incident harm saturates with fleet size. An independent exact simulation
validated every analytic result. Future work will lift the exponential
assumption to semi-Markov sojourns for heavy-tailed encryption and restore times,
embed an adaptive, state-triggered response policy, extend the model to
federations of interacting micro-datacenters, and incorporate the strategic
ransom-payment decision as a game against the modeled defender.
